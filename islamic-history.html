Early Islamic History

I’m interested in the relationship between the early Muslim community, the changing political world of the caliphates, and the preservation of religious knowledge.

The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ conveyed the Qur’an and taught the early Muslim community. The Hijrah from Mecca to Medina in 622 marks a turning point and provides the epoch of the Islamic calendar. The Companions transmitted what they had heard and seen, and students of the next generation learned from them in different centres.

The first caliphates

632–661 · Rashidun
The period of Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthmān and ʿAlī saw territorial expansion, major changes in government and internal conflict.
661–750 · Umayyads
Damascus became the centre of a caliphate extending across a wide range of linguistic and political regions. Arabic assumed a growing role in administration.
750–1258 · Abbasids in Iraq
Baghdad was founded in 762. Court patronage and the movement of scholars and books helped make Iraq a centre of classical Islamic civilization. The sack of Baghdad in 1258 ended the Abbasid caliphate there.

A geography of teaching

Medina, Mecca, Kufa, Basra, Damascus and later Baghdad figure in the movement of students and transmitters. The Tābiʿūn and later generations learned in teaching circles, travelled to hear reports, and passed knowledge on by oral instruction and writing. Biographical and hadith works let readers trace parts of those networks.

The Ahl al-Ḥadīth and debates over theology belong in this intellectual history alongside political chronology. Collecting reports, weighing their transmission and writing commentaries continued across changes in political rule.
