Family memory

The connection I share here comes from family history. Keeping this heritage visible means learning about Charrúa history, listening to descendants and recognising the continuity of Indigenous communities in the region.

Reading the historical term “bugre”

In Brazil the word bugre was an externally imposed label for Indigenous people and often carried derogatory force. It was applied to different peoples and never supplied the name of one Indigenous nation. I use Charrúa as the specific identification preserved in my family history.

Across the Río de la Plata

Charrúa history belongs to a region that now spans Uruguay and parts of southern Brazil and northeastern Argentina. The Río de la Plata basin and adjoining grasslands supported diverse Indigenous communities, with shifting alliances and ways of living. Today’s borders came later. Colonial records used labels inconsistently, and neighbouring peoples had distinct histories of their own.

European colonization brought land seizure, livestock expansion, new trade and warfare. Indigenous people adapted to these changes as they defended territory and negotiated survival. In independent Uruguay, state forces attacked Charrúa people at Salsipuedes in 1831. Survivors faced forced removal, exploitation and marginalization.

Survival, memory and continuity

Descendants and organizations continue to preserve family memory and press for recognition. Charrúa organizations participated in the campaign to recognize Paso de Salsipuedes as a site of memory. Their own accounts are an essential part of understanding what happened and what survives.
